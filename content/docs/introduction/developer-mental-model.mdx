If your background is WordPress themes and content sites, WooCommerce will
feel deceptively familiar — same admin, same hooks, same templates — and then
suddenly unfamiliar the moment you touch anything commerce-related. This
chapter is about the shift in thinking that closes that gap.

We introduce the idea here and link outward. Later steps of WooDoc expand
each layer in depth.

## A blog site is one dimension. A store is several.

On a brochure or blog site, most work is **one-dimensional**: content goes in,
markup comes out. Change the template, change the output.

A store forces you to think in **multiple dimensions at once**, because a
single visible thing is the result of business rules, a lifecycle, data, and
often an external service:

## The question is not "how do I change the output"

A theme developer asks:

> "How do I change the displayed price?"

A WooCommerce developer eventually learns to ask:

<Lifecycle
  steps={[
    'Which price am I changing — catalog display, cart line, or order line?',
    'Is it a display format change or an actual value change?',
    'If it is a value change, what recalculates because of it?',
    'What is the sanctioned hook or API for that, and at what layer?',
    'What are the consequences downstream — tax, totals, payment, reports, email?',
  ]}
/>

Because in WooCommerce, "the price" is not one thing in one place.

## One change ripples

Suppose you change a product's price. Here is the chain of things that can
depend on that value:

<Lifecycle
  steps={[
    'Product price',
    'Cart line subtotal',
    'Coupon / discount calculation',
    'Fees',
    'Tax calculation',
    'Shipping (if rules depend on cart value)',
    'Order total',
    'Payment amount charged',
    'Order + order item records',
    'Transactional email contents',
    'Reports and analytics',
  ]}
  caption="Not every change touches every step — but you should know which steps yours touches before you ship it."
/>

<SafetyLevel level="risky" title="Why the naive fix bites">
  Overriding the price in the template changes the *number on the screen* but
  not the *number WooCommerce calculates with*. The customer sees one price and
  is charged another. The correct layer for a value change is a pricing filter
  or the cart calculation stage — not the display template.
</SafetyLevel>

<VersionNote version="WooCommerce 11.0.1">
  Concretely: the catalog price a product reports is filtered by
  `woocommerce_product_get_price` (and `_get_regular_price` /
  `_get_sale_price`); the formatted catalog price HTML by
  `woocommerce_get_price_html`. The amount a cart line is *charged* is set by
  calling `set_price()` on the line's product object during
  `woocommerce_before_calculate_totals` — `woocommerce_cart_item_price` only
  changes what is *displayed*. Confusing the two is exactly the bug above.
</VersionNote>

## Three layers of "where a change belongs"

Every customization sits at one of three safety levels. WooDoc labels them
consistently:

<SafetyLevel level="recommended">
  **Recommended extension** — official hooks, filters, and APIs; supported
  template mechanisms; a properly structured plugin or `mu-plugin`. Survives
  updates. This is the default answer.
</SafetyLevel>

<SafetyLevel level="risky">
  **Possible but risky** — overriding a template by copying it into your
  theme, or replacing a whole subsystem. Sometimes the right call, but you now
  own that copy forever and must re-check it on every WooCommerce update.
</SafetyLevel>

<SafetyLevel level="dangerous">
  **Dangerous** — editing WooCommerce core files, or depending on private
  internal behavior that has no stability guarantee. Breaks on update, breaks
  silently, and breaks other people's expectations.
</SafetyLevel>

You will see these three labels throughout WooDoc. The recurring lesson:

> **"Just because I can change it there does not mean I should."**

## Think in the lifecycle, not the page

A brochure site has pages. WooCommerce has a **lifecycle** that pages are
windows into:

<Lifecycle
  steps={[
    'Catalog — products are displayed and queried',
    'Cart — a per-visitor, recalculated collection of items',
    'Checkout — address, validation, payment method, order creation',
    'Order — a persisted record with a status and a history',
    'Payment — authorization / capture, redirects, webhooks',
    'Fulfilment — stock, downloads, shipping, notifications',
  ]}
  caption="The high-level WooCommerce lifecycle. Most 'why is this happening?' questions resolve to 'which stage am I in, and what fires there?'"
/>

When something behaves unexpectedly, the first diagnostic question is usually
*which stage of this lifecycle am I in, and what code runs at that stage* —
not *which template rendered this*.

## What this means in practice

- **Locate before you edit.** Identify the layer and lifecycle stage first.
  The [Where Does This Come From?](/docs/introduction/where-does-this-come-from)
  pattern is built for exactly this.
- **Change values at the calculation layer, formatting at the display layer.**
  Do not confuse the two.
- **Prefer the highest-safety layer that can do the job.** Hook or filter
  first; template override only when a hook cannot reach it; never core.
- **Trace the ripple.** Before shipping, list what downstream steps your
  change affects and confirm each still behaves.
- **Respect classic vs Blocks.** The same customization can require different
  techniques depending on which cart/checkout implementation is active.

## Do not over-learn this yet

You do not need to memorize the full lifecycle now. The goal of this chapter
is only to install the *shape* of the problem:

> WooCommerce work is multi-dimensional. A change has a **layer**, a
> **lifecycle stage**, a **safety level**, and a **ripple**. Name all four
> before you write code.

Later steps of WooDoc document each stage — cart calculation, checkout,
order lifecycle, payment flow, email flow — in detail.
